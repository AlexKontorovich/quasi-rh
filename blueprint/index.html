A quasi-Riemann Hypothesis in \(\Re(s)>11/12\)

We seek cancellation in Möbius over \(\mathbb Q\): \(\displaystyle \sum_{n\asymp X}\mu(n)\) It is well known that a power savings here is equivalent to quasi-RH. But over \(\mathbb Q\) there are not enough harmonics. We therefore lift to the Eisenstein field \(K=\mathbb Q(\omega)\): \(\displaystyle M(X):=\sum_{\substack{n\in\mathcal O\\\mathcal N(n)\asymp X}}\mu_K(n),\) and aim to prove that \(M(X) \ll_\varepsilon X^{11/12+\varepsilon}\) for every \(\varepsilon>0\). Since \(\zeta_K(s)=\zeta(s)L(s,\chi_{-3})\), nonvanishing of \(\zeta_K\) forces nonvanishing of both factors. We deform the Eisenstein Möbius sum by sextic characters: \(\displaystyle M_h(X):=\sum_{\substack{n\in\mathcal O\\ n\asymp X}}\mu_K(n)\chi_n(h)\) and take a second moment: \begin{equation}\label{eq:mean-square} S(X,H):=\sum_{\substack{h\in\mathcal O\\ h\asymp H}}|M_h(X)|^2 \end{equation} We seek roughly square-root cancellation on average: \(\displaystyle S(X,H)\ll XH,\) ignoring epsilons. By itself, that is not enough, because \(M(X)\) only appears once in \(S(X,H)\). In the application, \(H=X^{1+\epsilon}\) will be a hair larger than \(X\), so even with square-root cancellation on average, dropping all but one term only shows the trivial bound: \(|M(X)|^2\le S(X,H)\ll X H \), which implies \(M(X)\ll (XH)^{1/2}\); this is epsilon worse than trivial - but in Sarnak's terminology, "pregnant". ("You have to be pregnant to deliver!") But sixth powers act as an amplifier. They recover (almost) the original sum: \(\displaystyle |M_{p^6}(X)-M(X)|\ll XH^{-1/6}\quad\bigl(p\text{ prime},\ \mathcal N(p^6)\asymp H\bigr)\). Since there are very roughly \(H^{1/6}\) (again, ignoring epsilons) such, we get, roughly, that \(H^{1/6}|M(X)|^2\le S(X,H)\ll X H \), and hence \(M(X)\ll X^{1/2}H^{5/12}\), which gives the main result.

Opening the square in (1), first separate the diagonal \(n=m\): its contribution is \(O(HX)\), already of the required size. To see the mechanism off the diagonal, ignore all but the other extreme, \((n,m)=1\). Reversing orders and applying Poisson in \(h\) (write \(L=X^2/H\) for the dual length) gives schematically \(\displaystyle S(X,H)\approx \frac1L\sum_{\ell\asymp L}\sum_{\substack{n,m\asymp X\\(n,m)=1}}\mu_K(n)\mu_K(m)\,\mathcal G_{n,m}(\ell),\) where the unnormalized sextic Gauss factor has the shape \(\displaystyle \mathcal G_{n,m}(\ell)=\sum_{a\bmod nm}\overline{\chi_m(a)}\chi_n(a)\,e_K\!\left(\frac{\ell a}{nm}\right).\) The Gauss–Jacobi identity absorbs the Möbius signs into the Gauss factors on both sides, leaving cubic Gauss sums and explicit phase factors. Write the resulting normalized cubic-Gauss coefficient as \(\displaystyle c_3(m):=\widetilde g_3(m)\,\overline{\frac{m}{|m|}}.\) Here \(\widetilde g_3(m)\) is the cubic Gauss sum normalized by the square root of the norm, and \(m\) is the chosen primary generator. After rearranging the phases, the \(n\)- and \(m\)-sums recombine heuristically into an absolute square: \(\displaystyle \begin{aligned} S(X,H)&\approx\frac{X}{L}\sum_{\ell\asymp L}\sum_{n,m\asymp X}\overline{\chi_m(\ell)c_3(m)}\,\chi_n(\ell)c_3(n)\\&=\frac{X}{L}\sum_{\ell\asymp L}\left|\sum_{m\asymp X}\chi_m(\ell)c_3(m)\right|^2.\end{aligned}\) Coprimality restrictions and the remaining phase separation are suppressed in this schematic. Normalizing the two Gauss factors contributes a factor \(X\), changing the prefactor \(1/L\) to \(X/L=H/X\). We now recognize \(c_3(m)\) as the squarefree Fourier coefficients of Kubota’s cubic theta series.

For each fixed \(\ell\), restore the cube multiples \(mj^3\) to obtain the completed twisted theta coefficients, which we denote schematically by \(\displaystyle \tau_\ell(r):=\sum_{\substack{mj^3=r\\m\ {\rm squarefree}}}\chi_m(\ell)c_3(m)\,w_\ell(j).\) The cube weights \(w_\ell(j)\) include the required twists and normalizations. Completion replaces the inner squarefree sum by \(\sum_{r\asymp X}\tau_\ell(r)\); Möbius inversion in the cube factor recovers the original sum. Thus the completed sums enter our second moment in the schematic form \(\displaystyle S(X,H)\approx\frac{H}{X}\sum_{\ell\asymp L}\left|\sum_{r\asymp X}\tau_\ell(r)\right|^2,\) with the cube-inversion sums and their scale changes left implicit. The theta series is constructed as the residue of a metaplectic Eisenstein series, whose meromorphic continuation is obtained by Selberg’s resolvent/Fredholm method. Taking the residue preserves its automorphic transformation law, giving the theta series its modularity. This classical construction is an important component of the formalization. Modularity of the completed theta series gives a Voronoi-type summation identity, whose main shape in this normalization is \(\displaystyle \sum_{r\asymp X}\tau_\ell(r)\ \approx\ \frac{X}{L}\sum_{m\asymp Y}\tau^\vee_\ell(m),\qquad Y=\frac{L^2}{X},\quad \ell\asymp L.\) Here \(\tau^\vee_\ell(m)\) denotes the normalized coefficients at the reflected cusps; the cusp sums and transformed smooth weights are implicit. The reflection kernel has scale \(X/\mathcal N(\ell)^2\), giving the dual norm length \(Y\). Substituting this transformation into the second moment gives \(\displaystyle S(X,H)\approx\frac{H}{X}\frac{X^2}{L^2}\sum_{\ell\asymp L}\left|\sum_{m\asymp Y}\tau^\vee_\ell(m)\right|^2.\) We then reopen the cube completion and separate the phases. The resulting quadratic energies have the schematic form \(\displaystyle S(X,H)\ \approx\ \frac{H}{X}\frac{X^2}{L^2}\sum_{\ell\asymp L}^{*}\left|\sum_{m\asymp Y}^{*}a_m\,\overline{\chi_m(\ell)}^{\,3}\right|^2,\qquad Y=\frac{L^2}{X}.\) The stars indicate admissible squarefree rows and columns; \(a_m\) are fixed coefficients within each block, with \(|a_m|\le1\). The sextic characters now occur cubed, so they are quadratic! The sharp quadratic large sieve gives, ignoring epsilon losses, \(\displaystyle \sum_{\ell\asymp L}^{*}\left|\sum_{m\asymp Y}^{*}a_m\,\overline{\chi_m(\ell)}^{\,3}\right|^2\ll(L+Y)\sum_m^{*}|a_m|^2\ll(L+Y)Y.\) Consequently, this heuristic gives \(\displaystyle S(X,H)\ll\frac{HX}{L^2}(L+Y)Y=H(L+Y)\ll HX,\) since \(1\le Y\le L\le X\) when \(H\) is just above \(X\). This explains the target bound. The full proof organizes the extra phases, common divisors, cube scales, and non-squarefree rows through a second Poisson passage and finite descent, obtaining \(S(X,H)\ll_\varepsilon XH\,X^\varepsilon\) in the required range. Sixth-power amplification then yields \(M(X)\ll_\varepsilon X^{11/12+\varepsilon}\).

01Set up

Fix the Eisenstein arithmetic

Lift the Möbius sum to the Eisenstein integers \(\mathcal O=\mathbb Z[\omega]\). We write the sum over primary generators, so that each admissible ideal appears once; \(\mu_K(n)\) means the Möbius coefficient of the principal ideal \((n)\).

\[ \mathcal N(n)=\mathcal N((n)),\qquad \mu_K(n)=\mu_K((n)),\qquad n\text{ primary}\iff n\equiv1\pmod{3}. \]

For elements coprime to \(3\), the primary condition selects exactly one representative among the six unit associates.

Lineage. The Eisenstein setting begins with Eisenstein’s 1844 work on cubic reciprocity; the ideal and \(L\)-function language comes from Dedekind and Hecke. The factorization \(\zeta_K(s)=\zeta(s)L(s,\chi_{-3})\) is classical. Specific to this proof. The Eisenstein lift is the entrance to this \(11/12\) argument.

02Set up

Build \(M_h(X)\) and its finite second moment

Now make every ingredient visible in the summand: primaryness, Möbius, the actual sextic twist, and a smooth cutoff supported in \([1/2,1]\). Thus only \(X/2\leq \mathcal N(n)\leq X\) contributes.

\[ M_h(X)=\sum_{\substack{n\ {\rm primary}\\\mathcal N(n)\le X}} \mu_K(n)\chi_n(h)W\!\left(\frac{\mathcal N(n)}X\right), \quad S(X,H)=\sum_{\mathcal N(h)\le H}V\!\left(\frac{\mathcal N(h)}H\right)|M_h(X)|^2. \]

The public \(S\) is deliberately a finite sum. At the precise boundary where Poisson is needed, compact support proves it equal to a whole-lattice tsum.

This makes the intro’s dyadic notation precise. The target is square-root cancellation on average, \(S(X,H)\ll_\varepsilon XH X^\varepsilon\), with \(H\) a little larger than \(X\).

Lineage. Deforming a problem in a family has a geometric analogy in Deligne’s proof of the Weil Riemann hypothesis, through a Lefschetz pencil and monodromy; that is an analogy, not an input here. The modern amplification line runs through Iwaniec, Duke–Friedlander–Iwaniec, and Iwaniec–Sarnak. New here. The specific sextic deformation and sixth-power rows that recover the original sum belong to this argument.

03Transform

Open the square and apply the first Poisson summation

Expand \(|M_h(X)|^2\) and separate the diagonal, which already contributes only \(O(HX)\). For the remaining pairs, reverse the row and column sums and run Poisson summation in \(h\). The coprime pairs illustrate the mechanism; common divisors and coprimality masks are retained in the full argument.

\[ \sum_{h\in\mathcal O}W_h\,\overline{\chi_I(h)}\chi_J(h) \quad\stackrel{\rm Poisson}{\longrightarrow}\quad \sum_{D\mid{\rm mask}}\frac{\mu_K(D)}{\mathcal N(D)} \sum_{\ell\in\mathcal O}\mathcal K_{I,J,D}(\ell). \]

For coprime columns of norm about \(X\), the modulus has norm about \(X^2\). Thus Poisson gives dual norm length \(L=X^2/H\) and prefactor \(H/X^2=1/L\) when the Gauss sums are unnormalized. The displayed kernel \(\mathcal K\) includes the Fourier weight and its normalization; zero frequencies and tails are tracked separately.

Precedents. Duke–Iwaniec (1990) pass from Poisson summation to Gauss–Ramanujan sums and bilinear estimates for half-integral-weight coefficients. Their 1993 cubic exponential/Kloosterman identity is another nearby precursor, but not an input here. A complementary automorphic model is Selberg’s Eisenstein-series proof of the prime number theorem: in the formulation recorded by Sarnak, the index-1 Ramanujan coefficient is \(c_q(1)=\mu(q)\). Selberg originated that argument; Sarnak’s contribution is its effective version and quantitative zero-free region.

04Transform

Use Gauss–Jacobi identities to expose cubic coefficients

The first Poisson summation produces sextic Gauss sums. The Gauss–Jacobi identity absorbs the squarefree Möbius signs on both sides into cubic Gauss coefficients. Explicit local phases and cross-character factors remain and must be separated.

Write \(c_3(m)=\widetilde g_3(m)\overline{m/|m|}\), where \(\widetilde g_3(m)\) is normalized by \(\sqrt{\mathcal N(m)}\). These are the squarefree cubic-theta coefficients. As in the intro, the separated \(n,m\) sums are modeled by an absolute square with inner sum \(\sum_{m\asymp X}\chi_m(\ell)c_3(m)\). Normalizing the two Gauss factors changes the outside factor from \(1/L\) to \(X/L=H/X\).

\[ \mu_K\!\left(\prod p_i\right) G\!\left(\prod \chi_{p_i}^{\,e_i}\right) =\left(\prod_{i\ne j}\chi_{p_i}^{\,e_i}(p_j)\right) \prod_i G_i^{\rm converted}. \]

Lineage. The local cubic Gauss-sum algebra goes back to Hasse’s 1950 treatment, in the form foregrounded by equation (2) of Heath-Brown’s 2000 cubic work. New here. This proof derives the exact mixed sextic/cubic identity that absorbs the Möbius factor.

05Transform

Complete to the full cubic theta series

For each fixed \(\ell\), restore the cube multiples \(r=mj^3\) to the inner squarefree sum. This produces the completed twisted coefficients \(\tau_\ell(r)\) from the intro. The exact object is written over ideals, with the twist denoted by \(\Psi\):

\[ T_\Psi(W,X)=\sum_{I}\sum_J \frac{c_\Psi(I)}{\sqrt{\mathcal N(I)}}\,w_\Psi(J)\, V^\ast\!\left(\frac{\mathcal N(I)\mathcal N(J)^3}{X}\right). \]

Here \(c_\Psi(I)\) is the twisted squarefree coefficient and \(V^\ast(y)=\sqrt y\,W(y)\). The original squarefree sum \(\mathcal C_\Psi(W,X)=\sum_I c_\Psi(I)W(\mathcal N(I)/X)\) is recovered by the exact cube-inversion formula

\[ X^{-1/2}\mathcal C_\Psi(W,X) =\sum_J\mu_K(J)w_\Psi(J)\, T_\Psi\!\left(W,\frac{X}{\mathcal N(J)^3}\right). \]

Thus completion enters \(S(X,H)\) through a weighted family of completed sums at cube-divided scales. The intro suppresses this family to show the main transformation; its scales and weights are retained in the estimates.

Lineage. Kubota created the metaplectic framework and constructed theta functions as residues of metaplectic Eisenstein series. Patterson made the cubic theta series explicit and computed its cubic-Gauss-sum Fourier coefficients; Heath-Brown–Patterson used this connection for Kummer sums. The precise completion and cube-factor reopening used here follow Dunn–Radziwiłł, with precedents in Patterson and Heath-Brown.

Prove modularity and reflect the cubic theta series

The cubic theta series is obtained as a residue of a metaplectic Eisenstein series. Selberg’s resolvent/Fredholm method supplies meromorphic continuation, and the residue inherits the automorphic transformation law. Its modularity then exchanges the completed coefficient sum for dual sums at reflected cusps.

In the intro’s normalization, with \(\ell\asymp L\), the reflection kernel has scale \(X/\mathcal N(\ell)^2\). The basic dual length is \(Y=L^2/X\), and the squared transformation factor is \(X^2/L^2\). Thus the completed \(\tau_\ell\)-energy becomes a reflected \(\tau^\vee_\ell\)-energy with prefactor \(HX/L^2\). Individual branches also carry cusp, divisor, and cube scales.

Lineage. Meromorphic continuation uses Selberg’s 1956 resolvent/Fredholm method. Kubota applied that machinery metaplectically; Patterson computed the cubic coefficients and cusps; Yoshimoto developed the twisted functional equations. The explicit level-aspect cubic Voronoi transformation used here is Dunn–Radziwiłł’s extension of Patterson and Yoshimoto.

The formal statement below packages this transformation for each fixed shell and twist. Its reflected fiber collects the cusp branches, weights, and local factors suppressed in the intro. The branch-decoding results supply this identity to the mean-square argument.

\[ T_{\Psi_{\rm row}}(W,X)= \bigl(4\,\Theta'^{-1}c_{\rm rad}\bigr)\, \operatorname{ReflectedFiber}(W,X). \]

Use the quadratic large sieve

For admissible squarefree rows of norm about \(L\) and columns of norm about \(Y\), the sharp quadratic large sieve gives

\[ \sum_{\ell\asymp L}^{*} \left|\sum_{m\asymp Y}^{*}a_m\chi_{2,m}(\ell)\right|^2 \ \ll_\varepsilon\ (LY)^\varepsilon(L+Y)\sum_m^{*}|a_m|^2. \]

Lineage. The large sieve originates with Linnik (1941). Heath-Brown proved the sharp self-dual quadratic-character inequality over \(\mathbb Q\), and Goldmakher–Louvel proved the number-field form used here over \(\mathbb Q(\omega)\).

For \(|a_m|\le1\), the intro’s heuristic therefore gives \(S(X,H)\ll (HX/L^2)(L+Y)Y=H(L+Y)\ll HX\), ignoring epsilon losses. The full proof must sum the separate blocks with their weights and restrictions.

The adjacent Lean statement now gives the sharp \(L+Y\) bound for the displayed finite quadratic energy. It is proved from finite_family_quadratic_large_sieve; the initial \(M+N^2\) estimate remains available in the expandable implementation details.

Amplify with sixth powers and extract \(11/12\)

The intro illustrates amplification with prime sixth powers. The formal assembly uses the same mechanism by averaging all \(h=b^6\) with \(b\) in a smooth norm annulus. A sextic character on such a row is exactly the coprimality indicator. Averaging this indicator produces the density \(d(I)=\prod_{P\mid I}(1-1/\mathcal N(P))\).

\[ \chi_I(b^6)=\mathbf 1_{(b,I)=1},\quad B=X^{(1+\theta)/6},\quad |M_{\rm dens}(X)|\ll X^{(11+5\theta)/12+\eta}. \]

Here \(B\) is the amplifier’s norm scale and \(M_{\rm dens}(X)\) is the smoothed admissible-ideal sum with coefficients \(\mu_K(I)d(I)\). Choose \(\theta=6\varepsilon/5\) and \(\eta=\varepsilon/2\); then \((11+5\theta)/12+\eta=11/12+\varepsilon\). The next step removes the density by an Euler correction.

Use Mellin inversion and restore the Euler factors

The all-sixth-powers amplifier bounds the density-weighted sum \(M_{\rm dens}(X)\). Mellin inversion turns this bound into an analytic function in \(\Re(s)>11/12\). The density totientDensity is then accounted for by an explicit Euler product.

An absolutely convergent eulerCorrection removes the density, while finiteEulerFactor restores the finitely excluded bad primes. The public \([1/2,1]\) convention is converted to the upstream \([1,2]\) Mellin convention by the harmless dilation \(x\mapsto2x\).
