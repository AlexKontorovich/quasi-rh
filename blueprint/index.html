Set up
01Set up

Fix the Eisenstein arithmetic

Work in the Eisenstein integers \(\mathcal O=\mathbb Z[\omega]\). The sum is written over elements because that is the notation we want to read, but every coefficient is the coefficient of the principal ideal \((n)\).

\[ N(n)=N((n)),\qquad \mu_K(n)=\mu_K((n)),\qquad n\text{ primary}\iff n\equiv1\pmod{3}. \]

For elements coprime to \(3\), the primary condition selects exactly one representative among the six unit associates.

02Set up

Build \(A_u(N)\) and its finite second moment

Now make every ingredient visible in the summand: primaryness, Möbius, the actual sextic twist, and a smooth cutoff supported in \([1/2,1]\). Thus only \(N/2\leq N(n)\leq N\) contributes.

\[ A_u(N)=\sum_{\substack{n\ {\rm primary}\\N(n)\le N}} \mu_K(n)\chi_n(u)W\!\left(\frac{N(n)}N\right), \quad S(N,H)=\sum_{N(u)\le H}V\!\left(\frac{N(u)}H\right)|A_u(N)|^2. \]

The public \(S\) is deliberately a finite sum. At the precise boundary where Poisson is needed, compact support proves it equal to a whole-lattice tsum.

03Transform

Open the square and apply the first Poisson summation

Expand \(|A_u(N)|^2\), reverse the row and column sums, and run Poisson summation in the Eisenstein variable \(u\). Coprimality masks are removed by inclusion–exclusion.

\[ \sum_{u\in\mathcal O}W_u\,\overline{\chi_I(u)}\chi_J(u) \quad\stackrel{\rm Poisson}{\longrightarrow}\quad \sum_{D\mid{\rm mask}}\frac{\mu_K(D)}{N(D)} \sum_{h\in\mathcal O}\mathcal K_{I,J,D}(h). \]

The zero frequency and the nonzero tail are tracked separately. The important structural fact is that the original moment has become a dual-frequency sum carrying finite Gauss factors.

04Transform

Use Gauss–Jacobi identities to expose cubic coefficients

First Poisson produces sextic Gauss sums. Cubic reciprocity and the normalized Gauss–Jacobi relation absorb the squarefree Möbius sign into those local factors.

After conversion, the coefficient is no longer an awkward Möbius-weighted product of local transforms: it is the canonical cubic Gauss coefficient that belongs in the cubic theta series.

\[ \mu_K\!\left(\prod p_i\right) G\!\left(\prod \chi_{p_i}^{\,e_i}\right) =\left(\prod_{i\ne j}\chi_{p_i}^{\,e_i}(p_j)\right) \prod_i G_i^{\rm converted}. \]

05Transform

Complete to the full cubic theta series

The transformed coefficient first appears with squarefree, divisor, unit, and ray restrictions. The completion layer puts back every missing local piece and packages the result into one full ideal-indexed theta object.

\[ T_\Psi(W,X)=\sum_{I}\sum_J \frac{c_\Psi(I)}{\sqrt{N(I)}}\,w_\Psi(J)\, V^\ast\!\left(\frac{N(I)N(J)^3}{X}\right). \]

The cube \(N(J)^3\) is the structural feature that later lets Möbius inversion reopen the completed series at cube scales.

06Transform

Reflect the cubic theta series

The modular transformation law moves the completed theta sum to a reflected cusp. Analytically this is the cubic analogue of a Voronoi formula: the original coefficients are replaced by explicit Fourier coefficients and reflected kernels.

In this repository the models are built below the old packaged zero-free theorem. The fixed-shell identity comes from the Poisson, Gauss-sum, and branch-decoding layers; it is then promoted to the canonical theta-model interface needed by the density argument.

\[ T_{\Psi_{\rm row}}(W,X)= \bigl(4\,\Theta'^{-1}c_{\rm rad}\bigr)\, \operatorname{ReflectedFiber}(W,X). \]

07Transform

Apply Poisson a second time

On the reflected side, the key simplification is elementary but decisive: the cube of a sextic character is quadratic. The second Poisson transform therefore produces a family that the quadratic large sieve can see.

\[ \chi_6^3=\chi_2,\qquad \sum_{z\in\mathcal O}{\rm mask}(z)\, \overline{\chi_T(z)}\chi_S(z)W_z \longrightarrow \sum_{k\in\mathcal O}\widehat W_k\, {\rm GaussRow}_{S,T}(k). \]

The theorem keeps the conductor, coprimality mask, Fourier scale, and active Gauss-row factor explicit.

08Transform

Use the quadratic large sieve

The transformed rows are now quadratic. Their diagonal correlations contribute the row length \(M\); the off-diagonal correlations contribute the square of the conductor scale \(N^2\).

\[ \sum_{z\in R}\left|\sum_{I\in F}a_I\,\chi_I(z)^3\right|^2 \ \ll\ (M+N^2)\sum_{I\in F}|a_I|^2. \]

This is the main analytic estimate after the second Poisson passage. Everything later is bookkeeping, descent, and choosing scales so that this gain survives.

09Close

Close the mean square at every positive radius

The completion and sieve estimates are iterated through a finite descent. The crucial modification for \(11/12\) is that the descent reserve is not frozen at \(1/20\): for any \(\theta>0\), Lean installs CanonicalDescentScale.ofReserve (θ / 2).

\[ \sum_{\substack{z\in T\\N(z)\le Z^{1+\theta}}}|A_z(Z)|^2 \ \ll_{\theta,\varepsilon}\ Z\cdot Z^{1+\theta}\cdot Z^\varepsilon. \]

Being able to make \(\theta\) arbitrarily small is what lets the later sixth-power average reach \(11/12+\varepsilon\).

10Close

Amplify with sixth powers and extract \(11/12\)

Put \(u=b^6\). A sextic character evaluated at a sixth power is exactly the indicator that \(b\) is coprime to the column. We average over all \(b\) in a smooth annulus, not just primes. Its main term is the density \(\prod_{P\mid I}(1-1/N(P))\).

\[ \chi_I(b^6)=\mathbf 1_{(b,I)=1},\quad Y=Z^{(1+\theta)/6},\quad |M(Z)|\ll Z^{(11+5\theta)/12+\eta}. \]

Choose \(\theta=6\varepsilon/5\) and \(\eta=\varepsilon/2\). Then \((11+5\theta)/12+\eta=11/12+\varepsilon\).

11Close

Use Mellin inversion and restore the Euler factors

The amplifier naturally produces Möbius times totientDensity. That is the correct coefficient, not an error to discard. Inverse Mellin continuation turns its smoothed bound into an analytic function in \(\Re(s)>11/12\).

An absolutely convergent eulerCorrection removes the density, while finiteEulerFactor restores the finitely excluded bad primes. The public \([1/2,1]\) convention is converted to the upstream \([1,2]\) Mellin convention by the harmless dilation \(x\mapsto2x\).

\[ (L(s,\chi)L(s,\chi_K))\, (E_S(s)E_{\rm dens}(s)\,\mathcal M(s)) =\widehat W(s). \]

Conclude zeta nonvanishing

Choose a compactly supported Mellin test whose transform is nonzero at the point in question. The corrected identity then says that a zero of \(L(s,\chi)\) in the half-plane would force that nonzero transform to vanish.

First Lean proves the statement for every Dirichlet character, away from its possible pole. Specializing to the trivial character modulo one identifies its \(L\)-function with the Riemann zeta function; \(s=1\) is handled separately.
